# Hypertrophy Programming Brief

## Big Picture

The strongest common thread across Stronger by Science, 3DMJ/Eric Helms, Menno Henselmans, and the primary literature is that hypertrophy programming should be organized around high-quality hard sets per muscle, distributed across the week in a way the lifter can recover from and progress.

Practical default:

- Count direct weekly hard sets per target muscle first.
- Use indirect volume as partial credit, not as a full replacement for targeted work.
- Prefer exercises that are limited by the target muscle, can be progressed, and can be performed close to failure without form collapse.
- Use intensity techniques like myo-reps, rest-pause, drop sets, and supersets mostly to save time or add stimulus-efficient isolation work, not as magic.

## Weekly Volume

Useful starting ranges:

- Most muscles: roughly 10-20 hard sets per week is a common evidence-based starting zone.
- Lower maintenance or minimum effective work: often below 10 sets per week, especially for advanced lifters maintaining a muscle.
- Higher specialization blocks: 20+ sets per week can work for some muscles and people, but should be added gradually and judged by performance, recovery, joint stress, and whether later sets are still high quality.

Counting rules:

- A hard set is usually a set within about 0-3 reps in reserve, with stable technique and the target muscle meaningfully loaded.
- For hypertrophy, Stronger by Science argues that hard sets per muscle are generally more useful than `sets x reps x load`.
- Menno Henselmans highlights that many lifters can benefit from more volume, but the ceiling is individual and exercise-dependent.
- 3DMJ emphasizes that low-quality volume often makes people think they need more work than they really do.

## Frequency

Practical default:

- Train most muscles at least 2 times per week.
- Use 3+ exposures when weekly volume is high, when a muscle performs poorly after too many same-session sets, or when technique practice matters.
- Frequency seems less magical when volume is equated, but higher frequency helps distribute volume into better sessions.

Programming implication:

- If a muscle needs 14-18 hard sets weekly, splitting them across 2-4 days is usually easier than crushing them in one day.
- Higher frequency should reduce per-session sets, not simply add more work everywhere.

## Rep Ranges And Failure

Rep range:

- Hypertrophy can occur across a wide range of loads and reps if sets are taken close enough to failure.
- Most practical work should live where the exercise feels stable and efficient: often 5-10 reps for heavy compounds, 8-15 for many machine/DB lifts, and 12-30 for isolation or joint-friendly work.

Failure:

- Isolation lifts can be taken to failure more often.
- Big technical compounds should usually stop at technical failure or with a small RIR buffer.
- If a set ends because of breathlessness, bracing fatigue, grip, or low back fatigue rather than the target muscle, it is a weaker hypertrophy set for that target muscle.

## Myo-Reps / Rest-Pause

Best use:

- Time-efficient hypertrophy work for low-risk isolation or stable machine exercises.
- Good candidates: lateral raises, curls, pushdowns, leg extensions, leg curls, calf raises, machine chest fly, cable work.
- Use sparingly on exercises with high systemic fatigue or technical risk.

Basic setup:

1. Do one activation set near failure, commonly 10-30 reps.
2. Rest briefly, commonly 10-30 seconds or about 3-5 deep breaths.
3. Perform mini-sets of about 2-5 reps.
4. Repeat mini-sets until reps drop meaningfully, form degrades, or the target number of mini-sets is reached.

Example:

```text
Cable lateral raise
Activation: 18 reps @ 1 RIR
Rest: 20 seconds
Mini-sets: 5, 5, 4, 3
Stop when the next mini-set would be below 3 clean reps.
```

Programming notes:

- One myo-rep cluster is not always equivalent to one straight set. For practical counting, log it separately, then treat it as roughly 2-3 hard-set-equivalents depending on activation-set difficulty, mini-set count, and recovery cost.
- Do not let myo-reps replace all straight-set progression. Keep some normal sets so load and rep performance remain easy to interpret.
- If load falls below roughly 30-40% 1RM or mini-sets become fewer than about 5-6 total reps per effort bout, 3DMJ suggests the hypertrophy rationale becomes less clear.

## Supersets

Best pairings:

- Antagonist or non-competing pairings: push/pull, biceps/triceps, leg extension/leg curl, calves/abs.
- Pair a demanding lift with a very low-interference accessory only if performance is stable.
- Use alternating sets with short transitions, then rest enough before repeating.

Avoid or use cautiously:

- Squat + row, deadlift + press, RDL + pull-up, or other pairings that stack bracing, grip, low back, or cardiovascular fatigue.
- Two exercises competing for the same prime mover if the goal is quality volume rather than a deliberate intensity technique.
- Pairings where the second exercise consistently loses reps, load, range of motion, or focus.

Practical template:

```text
A1. Chest-supported row: 8-12 reps
Rest 30-60 seconds
A2. Machine chest press: 8-12 reps
Rest 90-150 seconds
Repeat
```

This saves time while giving each local muscle group more rest than a same-muscle superset.

## Lengthened Partials And Partial-Rep Progression

Current evidence and coaching consensus are favorable toward training at long muscle lengths, but not every exercise is equally suited to partials.

Good uses:

- Add lengthened partials after full-ROM reps on stable exercises.
- Use them as a progression option when full reps stall but technique remains controlled.
- Favor exercises where the lengthened position is clear and loadable: incline curls, preacher curls, overhead triceps extensions, leg curls, leg extensions depending on machine setup, deep leg press/squat patterns if joints tolerate them, cable fly variations, calf raises.

Example progression:

```text
Incline DB curl
Goal: 8-12 full reps + lengthened partials
Week 1: 10 full + 3 partials
Week 2: 11 full + 3 partials
Week 3: 12 full + 4 partials
Week 4: increase load, return to 8-9 full + partials
```

Cautions:

- Do not count every partial as a full rep.
- Do not use partials to hide loss of full-ROM strength.
- Be careful with positions that irritate elbows, shoulders, hips, or knees.

## Indirect Volume / Partial Credit

Compound lifts train secondary muscles, but not always with enough range, proximity to failure, or limiting tension to count as full sets for those muscles.

Useful heuristic:

- Prime mover in a compound: 1.0 set credit if it is close to failure and limited by that muscle.
- Strong secondary mover: 0.25-0.5 set credit.
- Stabilizer or weakly challenged secondary muscle: 0-0.25 set credit.

Examples:

- Pull-up: lats/upper back get primary credit; biceps might get 0.25-0.5 set credit depending on grip, range, and whether elbow flexors are limiting.
- Bench press: chest gets primary credit; triceps and front delts may get 0.25-0.5 credit.
- Row: upper back/lats get primary credit; biceps get partial credit; spinal erectors may get fatigue cost without much hypertrophy credit if torso support is absent.
- RDL: hamstrings/glutes get primary credit; low back often gets fatigue cost, not necessarily desired hypertrophy volume.

Use indirect credit to prevent accidental overuse and to explain fatigue, but keep direct isolation work for muscles that are priorities or under-stimulated by compounds.

## Program Review Heuristics

When we audit the program, look for:

- Weekly set counts per muscle, including direct and partial-credit indirect work.
- Whether high-volume muscles are split across enough days.
- Whether supersets are antagonist/non-competing or are quietly degrading performance.
- Whether myo-reps are assigned to low-risk exercises.
- Whether compounds are limited by the intended target muscle.
- Whether progression is clear for straight sets, myo-rep clusters, and lengthened partials.
- Whether fatigue costs are accumulating in grip, elbows, low back, shoulders, or cardiovascular demand.
