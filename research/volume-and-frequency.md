# Volume And Frequency (Deep Dive)

This expands the volume/frequency section of the programming brief with the specific
evidence and the places where good sources disagree. The practical defaults in the
brief still hold; this file is for when we need to justify a number or resolve a
conflict.

## The Core Model: Weekly Volume Is A Dose, Per-Session Volume Has A Ceiling

Two findings have to be held together, and they explain almost every apparent
contradiction in the volume literature.

1. **More weekly hard sets generally means more growth, with diminishing returns.**
   Schoenfeld, Ogborn, and Krieger's 2017 meta-analysis found a dose-response
   relationship between weekly sets per muscle and hypertrophy, with a notable
   step up past ~10 sets/week. Some studies push much higher: Radaelli et al. (2015)
   and Schoenfeld et al. (2019) both found more growth at 45 vs 30 vs 15 sets per
   muscle per week.

2. **A single session can only absorb so much.** Menno Henselmans' synthesis is that
   **productive volume is roughly 9-13 hard sets per muscle per session**, after
   which added sets in that session do little (and may be net-negative via muscle
   damage and protein breakdown). Rat data show muscle protein synthesis signaling
   plateauing around the ~10-set mark per session.

Put together: the way to get high *weekly* volume is to spread it across more
*sessions*, not to pile it into one. This is the mechanistic reason frequency
"matters" — not because frequency is magic, but because it's how you buy more
weekly volume without blowing past the per-session ceiling.

### Why the "high volume is counterproductive" studies don't contradict this

Menno's key insight: the studies that found *worse* results from "high" volume
(Barbalho 2018/2019; Amirthalingam 2017 GVT; Heaselgrave 2019) all trained the
muscle **once or twice a week**, so their "high volume" groups were doing ~13-20
sets *in a single session* — past the per-session ceiling. They weren't testing
"too much weekly volume," they were testing "too much per session." The studies
that pushed weekly volume to 30-45 sets and still saw gains trained each muscle
**3x/week**, keeping per-session volume around 10. Same conclusion from both
directions.

## Volume Landmarks (RP / Israetel Framework)

Renaissance Periodization's landmarks are a useful vocabulary even if the exact
numbers are individual. They describe a per-muscle, per-week scale:

- **MV (Maintenance Volume):** least work that holds existing muscle. Often well
  below 10 sets/week, lower still for advanced lifters maintaining a muscle. Useful
  during cuts, busy periods, or for a muscle that isn't a priority.
- **MEV (Minimum Effective Volume):** least work that actually grows the muscle.
  Tends to rise with training age (a near-beginner may grow on 6-8 sets; an
  advanced lifter's MEV for a lagging muscle may be 10-14).
- **MAV (Maximum Adaptive Volume):** the range where you get the best growth per
  unit of recovery cost. This is where most weeks should live. It's a range, not
  a point, and it moves week to week.
- **MRV (Maximum Recoverable Volume):** the most you can recover from before
  performance and recovery degrade. You don't live here; you brush against it late
  in a mesocycle, then deload.

How they're used in practice: start a block near MEV, add a little volume each week
toward MAV/MRV (performance and recovery as the guide), then deload and reset. The
landmarks are estimates to be calibrated against your own performance, soreness, and
joint stress, not lookup-table truth.

### Where sources diverge on volume

- **SBS / Nuckols:** count weekly hard sets per muscle as the primary lever; be
  skeptical of over-precise per-set accounting (see effective-reps note). Broadly
  endorses the ~10-20 set/week working zone for most people.
- **Menno:** the *weekly* ceiling is higher than people think (some lifters benefit
  from 30+ sets), **but only if frequency is high enough to keep per-session volume
  in the 9-13 range.** Caps are per-session, not per-week.
- **RP / Israetel:** formalizes individual ceilings (MRV) and pushes
  autoregulating volume up and down within a mesocycle rather than holding it flat.
- **3DMJ / Helms:** warns that a lot of "I need more volume" is actually low-quality
  volume — junk sets, poor proximity to failure, or sets where the target muscle
  wasn't the limiting factor. Fix quality before adding quantity.

The honest synthesis: 10-20 hard sets/week is a safe default for most muscles;
some people and some specialization blocks justify more; the binding constraint is
usually per-session quality and recovery, not a magic weekly cap.

## Frequency

**When weekly volume is equated, frequency has a modest direct effect on
hypertrophy.** Meta-analytic data show little hypertrophy difference across 1-6
days/week *at equal weekly volume*. The clear, robust finding is narrower:
**2-3x/week beats 1x/week** when volume is equated — largely because cramming a
week's volume into one session pushes past the per-session ceiling and tanks set
quality. Comparisons of 2x vs 3x at equal volume tend to come out similar.

Practical reading:

- **Frequency is a tool for distributing volume, not an independent growth driver.**
  Its value rises as a muscle's weekly volume rises.
- Default to **2x/week** for most muscles. Go to **3-4x** when weekly volume is high
  (so per-session stays ~6-10 quality sets), when a muscle fades badly after several
  same-session sets, or when technique practice matters (big compounds).
- Higher frequency should mean **fewer sets per session**, not the same per-session
  volume repeated more often. The point is to keep every set high quality.
- Strength (as opposed to size) appears to benefit a bit more from higher frequency
  under volume-matched conditions — relevant if a lift's skill/strength matters, not
  just the muscle's size.

## Practical Decision Rules

- Set a weekly hard-set target per muscle (start 10-20 for priorities; less for
  maintenance).
- Divide by a per-session cap of roughly **6-10 quality sets** to decide how many
  sessions that muscle needs. If the math wants 16 sets, that's 2-3 sessions, not one.
- Add volume across a block toward the top of the range, judged by performance and
  recovery, then deload (see periodization note).
- If sets late in a session lose reps/load for reasons unrelated to the target muscle
  (cardio, grip, bracing, focus), you've hit the per-session ceiling — move volume to
  another day rather than grinding more sets.
