# Intensity Techniques (Deep Dive)

Myo-reps, rest-pause, drop sets, lengthened partials, and supersets. For each: what
it is, the evidence, when it actually pays off, and how to count it toward volume.
This expands the myo-reps/supersets/partials sections of the programming brief.

## The Shared Logic: Effective Reps And SFR

Most of these techniques rest on the **effective reps** idea: the reps that drive
the most growth are the hard ones near failure, where motor-unit recruitment is
maximal and bar speed slows. Intensity techniques are ways to **pack more effective
reps into less time** by reaching/returning to that high-effort zone repeatedly with
short rests.

The catch (SBS's "Evidence is Lacking for Effective Reps"): you can't count effective
reps precisely, and over-engineering around them is a mistake. Treat these techniques
as **time-efficient ways to accumulate quality stimulus on low-fatigue exercises** —
not as mechanically superior to straight sets. Their best use is dictated by
**stimulus-to-fatigue ratio (SFR)**: apply them where stimulus is high and systemic/
joint fatigue is low (isolation, machines), not on big compounds where the added
fatigue and technical risk wreck the SFR.

## Myo-Reps (Borge Fagerli)

**Origin:** developed by Borge Fagerli (~2006, refined 2008), built on the
effective-reps premise — only the last several reps of a set, near failure, do most
of the growth work, so cluster many of those reps together efficiently.

**Standard setup:**
1. **Activation set** to or near failure, commonly **12-20 reps** (the full method
   spans ~9-40 depending on load, typically 30-50% 1RM). This pre-fatigues the muscle
   so subsequent short efforts are all in the effective zone.
2. **Brief rest:** ~3-5 deep breaths (~10-30 seconds).
3. **Mini-sets** of ~3-5 reps.
4. **Repeat** mini-sets until reps drop below target or form degrades (typically
   3-5 mini-sets).

**Best candidates:** lateral raises, curls, pushdowns, leg extensions, leg curls,
calf raises, cable fly, machine isolation — low technical risk, low systemic fatigue,
muscle is the limiting factor.

**Poor candidates:** squats, deadlifts/RDLs, heavy rows, heavy overhead press —
the short rests stack cardiometabolic and bracing fatigue and the muscle often
isn't what fails first. 3DMJ's rest-pause/myo-reps article makes this exact point:
keep short-rest methods on non-tiring isolation work, not on compounds.

**Counting:** one myo-rep cluster ≠ one straight set. For program accounting, log it
separately and treat it as roughly **2-3 hard-set-equivalents** depending on
activation difficulty and mini-set count. Don't let myo-reps replace all straight-set
progression — keep some normal sets so load/rep performance stays interpretable.
If load drops below ~30-40% 1RM, the hypertrophy rationale gets shakier.

## Rest-Pause

Closely related to myo-reps but usually with a **heavier load** and slightly longer
intra-set rests (~15-30s), reaching failure or near-failure each cluster. DC training
is the aggressive, to-true-failure cousin; Fagerli's myo-reps deliberately use
stopping criteria to stay just shy of repeated absolute failure and manage fatigue.

Use rest-pause for the same low-fatigue, stable exercises as myo-reps. The practical
difference is mostly load and how hard each cluster bites. Same counting caution:
it's a stimulus-dense effort bout, not a free extra set.

## Drop Sets

Reach failure, immediately strip load, continue to failure again, repeat for 2-3
drops with minimal rest. Meta-analyses (e.g. the 2023 drop-set review) find drop sets
produce **similar hypertrophy to traditional sets in much less time** — they're a
**time-efficiency tool, not a superiority tool**. A newer 2026 review continues to
examine the method.

Best on machines/cables/dumbbells where stripping load is fast and safe (leg
extension, lateral raise, pushdown, machine press). Avoid on barbell compounds (slow,
risky load changes; high fatigue). Count similarly to a dense cluster: more stimulus
than one straight set, but discounted by the very low loads on the final drops.

## Lengthened Partials / Long-Muscle-Length Training

**What the evidence actually says:**

- Training at **long muscle lengths** (and partials biased to the stretched portion)
  produces **modestly more hypertrophy** than full ROM in several studies. A recent
  meta-analysis found a **small advantage (~+6-7% growth vs full ROM)**, with 3 of 4
  direct comparisons favoring lengthened partials.
- **But the mechanism label is contested.** SBS ("Do Lengthened Partials Really
  Stimulate Stretch-Mediated Hypertrophy?") argues most lengthened-partial training
  studies are **not** actually demonstrating "stretch-mediated hypertrophy" — the
  training stretch stimulus is far weaker than the long-duration static stretching in
  the animal models where SMH was defined. The growth edge is real but small, and
  calling it "stretch-mediated" likely misattributes the mechanism. SBS's own point:
  with an effect this small (~6.76%), a modest mechanism is plausible, so don't
  over-hype it either way.

**Practical takeaway:** favoring the lengthened position is a **reasonable, low-cost
choice** — a small, defensible edge — not a game-changer. Pick exercises where the
stretched position is clear, loadable, and joint-safe (incline/preacher curls,
overhead triceps extensions, leg curls/extensions, cable fly, calf raises, deep leg
press/squat if joints tolerate).

**Using partials specifically:** add lengthened partials *after* full-ROM reps, or as
a progression when full reps stall while technique holds. Don't count a partial as a
full rep, and don't use partials to paper over lost full-ROM strength.

```text
Incline DB curl
Goal: 8-12 full reps + lengthened partials
Wk1: 10 full + 3 partials   Wk2: 11 full + 3 partials
Wk3: 12 full + 4 partials   Wk4: +load, back to 8-9 full + partials
```

## Supersets

**Evidence (SBS):** agonist-antagonist (and non-competing) supersets save
substantial time with **similar hypertrophy outcomes** to traditional sets in the
relevant study, with cautions at high volumes and on taxing technical lifts.

**Good pairings (each muscle rests while the other works):** push/pull, biceps/
triceps, leg extension/leg curl, calves/abs, chest-supported row + machine press.

**Avoid:** pairings that stack bracing/grip/low-back/cardio fatigue (squat+row,
deadlift+press, RDL+pull-up), or two lifts competing for the same prime mover (unless
that's a deliberate pre-exhaust technique). Red flag: the second lift consistently
loses reps/load/ROM for reasons unrelated to its target muscle.

```text
A1. Chest-supported row: 8-12   (rest 30-60s)
A2. Machine chest press: 8-12   (rest 90-150s)
Repeat
```

## When To Reach For Each (Quick Guide)

- **Short on time, isolation work:** myo-reps, rest-pause, or drop sets on
  machines/cables.
- **Short on time, two muscles:** antagonist supersets.
- **Want a small ROM-based edge:** bias the lengthened position; add lengthened
  partials on stable lifts.
- **Big compounds / heavy / technical:** straight sets with full rest. Keep
  intensity techniques off these.
- **Always:** apply techniques to high-SFR exercises, log them separately, and don't
  let them replace interpretable straight-set progression on your key lifts.
