# Exercise Selection And Stimulus-To-Fatigue Ratio (Deep Dive)

How to choose exercises per muscle. The volume/frequency note assumes you have good
exercises to spend sets on — this note is how you pick them. The organizing concept
is **stimulus-to-fatigue ratio (SFR)**, plus resistance profile and where in the ROM
the exercise loads the muscle.

## Stimulus-To-Fatigue Ratio (SFR)

**Definition (coined by Israetel/RP):** the ratio of useful training stimulus for a
target muscle to the total fatigue the exercise imposes — local (muscle, joints,
connective tissue) and systemic (cardio, bracing, CNS). You want **high stimulus to
the target muscle, low fatigue everywhere else.**

Why it matters: high-SFR exercises let you do **more productive volume and recover
faster**, which feeds back into the volume/frequency math — you can train the muscle
more often because each session costs less. Low-SFR exercises (heavy deadlifts for
"back," barbell back squat purely for quads) deliver stimulus but at a fatigue cost
that limits how much else you can do.

### What lowers an exercise's SFR

- High systemic/cardio demand (the set ends from breathlessness, not the muscle).
- Heavy axial/low-back loading and bracing cost (free barbell work on the spine).
- High skill, coordination, or stability demand (a chunk of effort goes to balance,
  not the target muscle).
- Large load on vulnerable joints in poor positions.
- The set being limited by **grip, low back, or stabilizers** rather than the target.

### Rough fatigue ordering (general rule of thumb)

Barbell free-weight > dumbbell > cable > fixed machine, for the *same movement*. As
you move toward machines/cables, you usually keep most of the stimulus while shedding
stability and systemic fatigue cost — i.e. SFR rises. This is why machines and cables
are excellent for **high-volume and high-frequency** work and for the back half of a
session.

This does **not** mean machines are "better" — free-weight compounds carry strength,
loadability, and (for some) better real-world carryover. It means: spend your
fatigue budget deliberately. Use a few high-stimulus/high-fatigue compounds where
they earn it, and fill volume with high-SFR machine/cable/isolation work.

## Resistance Profile (Where The Exercise Is Hard)

The **resistance profile** is where in the range of motion an exercise loads the
muscle most. It interacts with the lengthened-position findings (see intensity-
techniques note): exercises that load the muscle hard **in the stretched/lengthened
position** appear to deliver more stimulus per set.

- **Loaded in the stretch (favorable):** lying/seated leg curl (hamstrings), incline
  and preacher curls (biceps), overhead triceps extensions, cable fly / pec deck,
  deep leg press, RDLs (hamstrings). These tend to be high-stimulus picks.
- **Loaded in the shortened/contracted range only:** many "peak contraction" machine
  movements, concentration-style curls, kickbacks. Not useless, but as a muscle's
  *primary* movement they leave the stretched position under-loaded.
- **Practical move:** make sure each muscle has at least one exercise that challenges
  it in the lengthened position, then add others for contracted-range or mid-range
  emphasis if desired. Variation across the strength curve is a reasonable way to
  cover a muscle fully.

Free-weight resistance profiles change with the angle of the load (gravity is
vertical), so a free-weight movement is often hardest at one specific joint angle and
easy elsewhere; cables/machines can keep tension more constant or bias it toward the
stretch. That's another reason cable/machine variants often have favorable profiles.

## Criteria For Picking An Exercise (Checklist)

For a given muscle, prefer exercises that are:

1. **Limited by the target muscle** — the muscle fails first, not grip/back/cardio.
2. **Loadable in the lengthened position** — challenges the stretch, not just the
   squeeze.
3. **Full, reproducible ROM** — same range every rep so progression is real.
4. **Progressible** — small load or rep increments are available (machines/cables/DBs
   help here; tiny-plate barbell work too).
5. **Stable / low skill ceiling for the goal** — minimal effort lost to balance.
6. **Joint-friendly for you** — no position that reliably irritates the elbow,
   shoulder, hip, knee, or low back.
7. **High SFR overall** — strong stimulus, low collateral fatigue.

No exercise maxes every criterion; the heavy compounds trade SFR for loadability and
strength carryover. Build each muscle's menu as a **mix**: one or two anchor
movements (often a compound or a high-stimulus lengthened-position lift) plus
high-SFR accessories to add volume cheaply.

## Putting A Muscle's Exercises Together

A workable template per muscle:

- **1 anchor** loaded in the stretch and progressible (often the muscle's "big"
  movement).
- **1-2 accessories** with high SFR (machine/cable/DB) to add quality volume across
  the week without piling on fatigue.
- Reserve **intensity techniques (myo-reps/drops)** for the high-SFR accessories, not
  the anchor (see intensity-techniques note).
- Cover the **lengthened position** with at least one pick; optionally add
  contracted/mid-range emphasis for completeness.

Example (side delts): anchor = a stable lateral raise variant loaded through range
(cable, leaning, or machine, which keeps tension in the stretched/bottom position);
accessory = a second lateral raise angle or a reverse-fly bias; intensity technique =
myo-reps on the cable raise late in the session. Avoid leaning on overhead press
*alone* for side-delt size — its SFR for the side delt specifically is mediocre and
its fatigue cost is high.

## How This Feeds The Program Audit

When reviewing the routine (see program-audit-checklist), for each exercise ask:

- Is the **target muscle the limiting factor**, or is it grip/back/cardio? (low SFR
  flag)
- Does the muscle get **at least one lengthened-position** loaded exercise?
- Are **high-fatigue compounds** doing work that a higher-SFR option could do more
  cheaply — or are they justified by strength/loadability goals?
- Is **volume being spent on low-SFR exercises** that limit frequency and recovery?
- Are **intensity techniques** on the high-SFR accessories rather than the anchors?

## 2025 Update: Full ROM vs Lengthened Partials

The lengthened-partial / stretch-position hype is real but has cooled at the edges,
and the practical takeaway has converged on **full ROM through a real stretch**:

- **Milo Wolf** — still all-in: ~50% of reps as long-length partials, expects ~5-10%
  more growth. Best candidates: lateral raises, curls, pushdowns, most back work, flyes.
- **Jeff Nippard** — ranks exercises largely *by* stretch + tension at the bottom; cites
  a 2023 study where overhead triceps ext grew the long head ~40% more than pushdowns.
- **Menno Henselmans (2025)** — flags a 2025 study where lengthened-*bias* training showed
  no significant advantage over shortened over 10 weeks, concluding **full ROM matters more
  than chasing peak stretch tension**. Also re-confirmed agonist-antagonist supersets save
  time with no hypertrophy penalty.

**Working rule for this program:** pick exercises with a **full, loadable ROM that passes
through a real stretch** (this captures ~95% of the agreed-on benefit). Treat lengthened
partials as an **optional finisher on isolations**, not a mandate. Ryan's call (2026-07-04):
**prioritize ROM over partials.**

## Gear-Specific Picks (Ryan's Home Gym)

See [equipment.md](equipment.md). The catch: most creators' #1 pick per muscle is a cable or
machine (Nippard's best side delt = cable lateral raise; best chest = machine press / seated
cable fly; best biceps = face-away Bayesian cable curl; best triceps = overhead cable ext),
but Ryan has **ONE cable and no machines**. Translation rule: **give the single cable to the
movement that most needs it each session; use DB stretch-equivalents everywhere else.**

- **Side delts (V-taper priority):** cable lateral raise (the cable slot) + **side-lying DB
  raise** (stretch-at-bottom, braced on incline bench, no cable) + standing DB raise for volume.
- **Back/lat width:** weighted pull-ups (wide/neutral) + lat pulldown (cable slot) + **DB
  pullover** (loads lat lengthened, frees the cable) + chest-supported DB row for thickness.
- **Rear delt:** **prone incline chest-supported DB reverse fly** (arms hang off the pad edges
  — solves the SelectTech-width / foam-block problem) is the primary; single-arm cable reverse
  fly or a **low-pulley** seated rope face pull are cable alternatives (the rack's high anchor
  is too steep for standard face pulls).
- **Chest:** barbell bench + incline DB press + DB fly (stretch). Dips are a **future anchor**
  (Ryan ~1 rep now); build bodyweight reps with a forward lean before loading.
- **Biceps:** incline DB curl (the DB equivalent of the Bayesian stretch) + hammer/DB curls.
- **Triceps:** **overhead DB or rope extension as the anchor** (long-head stretch; ~40%>pushdown),
  pushdown as accessory. Overhead DB is elbow-friendlier than skullcrushers.
- **Quads:** back squat + Bulgarian split squat (ATG) + heels-elevated goblet. No leg extension.
- **Hams/glutes:** RDL, deadlift, single-leg DB RDL (hinge only) + **DB/barbell hip thrust**
  (Menno rates hip thrust ≈ squat for glutes) for the thick-legs goal.
- **Calves:** standing DB (gastroc) + seated DB (soleus, bent-knee).
